AI-Powered Job Search System

A complete walkthrough — how to automate your job hunt end to end
What this is: A real system, built by one person managing a job search alongside a full-time job. It wakes up every morning, scans job boards, filters for relevant roles, generates tailored application materials using AI, sends a digest email, and updates a personal dashboard. The human only does three things: maintain the hitlist, review the materials, and click submit. All personal details have been replaced with placeholders.

Who this is for: Anyone who wants to replicate or adapt this system. Each section explains both what the system does and why it was built that way.

The core idea
A typical job search is bottlenecked by two things: discovery (finding out roles exist) and preparation (writing tailored materials). Both are things AI can do faster and more systematically than a person scanning job boards manually. This system delegates both. The human's job shifts to judgment: is this role worth my time? Does this cover letter sound like me? That's the part that actually requires a person — and it's the only part that scales with your seniority, not your free time.
Part 1

1 The Company Hitlist

A curated list of target companies. The source of truth for everything the scanner monitors.
Why a hitlist instead of searching job boards?
Job boards are noisy and algorithmic. This system inverts the model: you decide in advance which companies are worth your attention, then watch only those. The list is intentionally curated — a company on the list means you've decided you'd seriously consider working there. This makes the downstream materials better (the AI knows the context is genuine interest) and filters out noise before it reaches you.

Each company in the hitlist is a JSON object. The fields are:

{ "name": "[Company Name]", "ats": "greenhouse", // which ATS they use: greenhouse | lever | ashby | custom "slug": "[their-ats-slug]", // the identifier in the ATS API URL "careers_url": "https://...", // their careers page (for reference + custom ATS fallback) "why": "[Why this company is interesting to you]", "verified": true // have you confirmed the ATS slug actually works? }

How to find the ATS slug

  • Greenhouse: Look at any job URL — it's usually boards.greenhouse.io/slug/jobs or embedded in the careers page source.
  • Ashby: Check jobs.ashbyhq.com/slug — the slug is the company's unique Ashby identifier.
  • Lever: Job URLs are jobs.lever.co/slug/... — slug is right there in the path.
  • Custom ATS: No API available. Mark as "ats": "custom" — the scanner will skip it and remind you to check manually.

How to decide who goes on the list

  • Would you accept an offer from them today? If not, don't add them.
  • Do they hire people in your function in your target region?
  • Are they at a stage where they'd need someone like you? (Too early = no budget. Too late = no equity.)
  • Re-audit the list monthly. Companies fall off if they freeze hiring; new ones get added as you learn about them.
Design thinking — why tag the ATS type?
Each ATS has a different API shape. Greenhouse uses /v1/boards/{slug}/jobs. Lever uses /v0/postings/{slug}?mode=json. Ashby uses /posting-api/job-board/{slug}. Without knowing which ATS a company uses, you'd have to try all three. Tagging upfront means the scanner calls exactly one endpoint per company — no guessing, no wasted requests. The ATS tag also feeds the resume adapter downstream.

The two-filter system

Every job found by the ATS API must pass two filters before the AI tailor is called:

Filter 1 — Role title keywords

# What counts as a relevant role ROLE_KEYWORDS = [ "[function keyword 1]", # e.g. "go-to-market" "[function keyword 2]", # e.g. "growth lead" "[function keyword 3]", # e.g. "head of marketing" # ... 10-20 variants of your target function ]
How to build your keyword list
Start with the 3 most common titles for your target role. Then add: seniority variants (Lead, Head of, Director, VP), function synonyms (GTM vs Growth vs Commercial vs Revenue), and regional variants (APAC, SEA, Asia). Err on the side of broad — a false positive caught by the AI is fine; a missed role is not.

Filter 2 — Location

# Locations to include INCLUDE = [ "[target city/region]", "[target country]", "remote", "hybrid" ] # Locations to explicitly exclude EXCLUDE = [ "[city 1]", # e.g. "san francisco" "[city 2]", # e.g. "new york" "remote - us", # region-specific remote "remote - emea", ]
Why a separate exclude list?
"Remote" alone isn't enough — many companies post "Remote" jobs that are US-only in practice. Explicitly excluding US and EU cities surfaces only genuinely global or target-region remote roles. The include list catches your target location; the exclude list removes false positives.

Hiring news monitoring

The scanner also queries Google News RSS for each company in your hitlist, looking for articles about hiring, expansion, or new offices in your target region. This surfaces signals before roles are even posted — useful for reaching out proactively.

# News is relevant if the article title contains: # (1) the company name AND # (2) a location term (your target region) AND # (3) a signal term (hiring, expand, headcount, etc.) query = f'"{company_name}" [target city]' url = f'https://news.google.com/rss/search?q={query}&hl=en-SG&gl=SG' # Filters to last 14 days only
Why monitor news alongside jobs?
A company announcing a new Singapore office this week will have open roles in 4-8 weeks. If you've already connected with someone there by then, you're not a cold applicant — you're a warm referral. The news feed is an early-warning system for relationship-building.

Scheduling the scan

The script runs daily via launchd (macOS) or cron (Linux). You set it once and forget it.

macOS — launchd plist

<key>StartCalendarInterval</key> <dict> <key>Hour</key><integer>8</integer> <key>Minute</key><integer>0</integer> </dict>
Install: launchctl load ~/Library/LaunchAgents/com.yourname.jobsearch.plist

Linux / server — cron

# Runs at 8am daily 0 8 * * * cd /path/to/job-search && \ python3 scripts/daily_scan.py \ >> scan.log 2>&1
Add via: crontab -e
Part 3

3 The Master Resume & AI Tailor

One source file. Claude reads it and the job description, then generates everything — tailored per role.

The master resume — a tagged bullet bank

The master resume is not a finished resume. It's a complete record of everything you might want to say — more than fits on a page. Each bullet is tagged so the AI knows when it's allowed to use it:

Tag types

TagMeaning
[resume]Eligible for the tailored resume
[cover-letter-only]Cover letter only — never in resume
[both]Either medium — rephrase to fit
[linkedin]Profile copy only

Why tag bullets?

Design thinking
Some things sound good in a cover letter ("I built this from scratch because I believed in the mission") but are too narrative for a resume. Tags prevent the AI from using cover-letter material as a bullet point — and vice versa. The master file is the single source of truth; the tags are routing metadata that tell downstream steps what's fair game.
# Example master resume structure (Markdown) ## Experience ### [Company Name] — [Your Title] (Start Date – End Date) - [resume] [theme-tag] Led X initiative across N markets, achieving $Y outcome. Metric-first, specific. - [resume] [theme-tag] Built and scaled Z programme from 0→1, owning relationships with [stakeholder type]. - [cover-letter-only] More narrative context about why this role mattered to me personally — too wordy for a resume bullet. - [both] Cross-functional initiative that works in either medium with different phrasing. ## Summary variants # Keep 2-3 summary paragraphs tuned for different role types [summary-gtm] GTM-focused summary opener... [summary-bd] Business development-focused opener...

Where the master file lives — Obsidian as the writing environment

The master resume doesn't live only in the project folder. The primary copy lives inside an Obsidian vault, synced via iCloud. The scanner checks the Obsidian path first; if it's not found (different machine, Obsidian not installed), it falls back to the project-local copy.

Two-tier source of truth

# Scanner load order # 1. Primary — Obsidian vault (iCloud-synced) OBSIDIAN_PATH = Path( "~/Library/Mobile Documents/" "iCloud~md~obsidian/Documents/" "[Vault Name]/[Folder]/career-master.md" ) # 2. Fallback — project-local backup LOCAL_PATH = Path( "data/resumes/[your_name]_base.md" )

Why Obsidian?

Obsidian is a native Markdown editor with a better writing UX than a code editor. You get search, tag filtering, backlinks, and a distraction-free canvas for writing and editing bullet content. The file is still plain Markdown — the scanner reads it as text, no Obsidian-specific parsing needed.

Keeping the career master in Obsidian also means it lives alongside your other career notes — company research, interview prep, salary benchmarks — in one connected knowledge base.

The editing workflow
You open Obsidian, edit career-master.md, and save. iCloud syncs the file. The next time the scanner runs, it reads the updated version from the iCloud path — no copy-paste, no manual sync step. The scanner never writes to this file, only reads from it, so there's no risk of the automation overwriting your edits.
When to update the master file
  • After finishing a project at work — add 2-3 fresh bullets while the details are in your head.
  • After an interview — add the framing that resonated with the interviewer.
  • After reading a JD that made you realize you're underselling something — add a bullet that covers that angle.
  • Quarterly review: archive bullets older than 5 years or from roles you no longer want to lead with.
The more bullets exist, the better the AI can tailor. Thin source material = generic output.
The fallback matters. If you're running the scanner on a server or a machine without Obsidian installed, the local data/resumes/ copy is what gets used. Keep it in sync with the Obsidian version — or point the fallback path at the iCloud Drive location directly, since iCloud files are accessible as regular file paths on macOS.

What Claude generates per role

One API call. Five outputs. All specific to the job description and the company.

1. Tailored Resume (Markdown)
~450–600 words. Picks the 3–5 most relevant roles, selects bullets tagged [resume] or [both] that match the JD, rewrites the summary to match the role's framing. Skills section resized to ATS spec.
2. Cover Letter
150–220 words. Three paragraphs: lead with your strongest relevant credential, a specific achievement that maps to what this role needs, why this company specifically. No "I am writing to apply" openers.
3. Resume Angle
2–3 sentences explaining the editorial decision behind the resume — which framing was chosen and why. Helps you understand the AI's reasoning and catch cases where it chose wrong.
4. Fit Notes
1–2 sentences. An honest read on fit: where you're strong vs. where you're a stretch. Helps you decide whether to invest time in this application.
5. Networking Targets
2 job titles at the company worth connecting with before or after applying — with a rationale for each. Seeds your outreach list before you've even submitted.
The prompt structure
The prompt sends: your full master resume, the job description, and the company name. It asks for all five outputs as a single JSON object. Structured JSON output means the script can parse each field independently without post-processing.
Why structured JSON output? The script needs to write each piece (resume, cover letter, notes) to separate files. Asking Claude to return JSON with named fields makes parsing deterministic — no regex hunting through prose output. The script does json.loads(response) and indexes directly into result["tailored_resume_md"].

Application folder structure

Each matched role gets its own folder. Everything related to that application lives there.

data/applications/ └── <company>-<role-slug>/ ├── resume.md # tailored Markdown resume (Pass 1 output) ├── cover-letter.md # 150-220 word cover letter └── notes.md # resume angle + fit notes + networking targets + JD snapshot
Part 4

4 The Dashboard & Email Digest

How the job seeker actually sees and uses the system. Two surfaces: a local HTML file and a morning email.

The dashboard

A single HTML file, regenerated every time the scanner runs. No server required — just open it in a browser. Five sections:

  • Stats bar — new today / total tracked / applied / networking contacts. Four numbers at a glance.
  • Alert banner — if new roles were found today, they surface at the top with a visual callout. Acts within 24 hours = better application.
  • All Tracked Roles — a table sorted by date posted. Each row has the role, company, location, status, and a link to its application materials.
  • Application Materials — accordion panels, one per role. Expand to see the tailored resume, cover letter, fit notes, and networking targets. Copy button on each.
  • Hiring News — articles from the last 14 days about your target companies expanding or hiring in your region. Sorted by date.
  • Networking CRM — a lightweight contacts table. Name, title, company, LinkedIn, last contact date, status. Add contacts from the dashboard via a form (outputs a terminal command to run if no server is running).
  • Company Hitlist — the full list of companies being monitored, with their ATS type and why you added them.
  • Status updates are visual-only in the dashboard. To persist a status change (e.g. "reviewing" → "applied"), edit jobs.json directly.
The intended daily workflow
Takes under 15 minutes on a busy weekday.
  1. Open email. Scan subject line for new role count.
  2. If new roles: open dashboard. Read the fit notes for each. Decide which to pursue (often 1 of 3).
  3. For roles you're pursuing: expand the accordion, copy the cover letter, review the tailored resume, open the application folder.
  4. Check networking targets. If you know someone at the company, reach out today. If not, send a cold note this week.
  5. Submit the application — upload the ATS-specific file from Part 5.

The email digest

Sent only when something new was found. Subject line tells you immediately whether to pay attention.

When a digest is sent

Subject: [Job Alert] N new roles found today

Body includes: each new role with company, title, location, and direct URL. A reminder to open the dashboard for full materials.

Also includes any new hiring news articles found that day.

When no digest is sent

If the scan finds zero new roles and zero new news articles, no email is sent. Silence = nothing to do. No daily "no news" emails cluttering your inbox.

The dashboard is still regenerated — you can always open it to see the full picture.

Design thinking — why email instead of a notification?
Email is ambient. A push notification on your phone at 8am while you're getting ready creates cognitive load at the wrong moment. An email sits in your inbox, available when you're ready to act. It also creates a natural paper trail — you can search your inbox to find "when did I first see this role?" The digest is designed to be a briefing, not an interrupt.

Setting up email sending

The system sends to itself — the same address is sender and recipient. For iCloud Mail, you need an app-specific password (iCloud generates one at appleid.apple.com). For Gmail, use an App Password or OAuth. The SMTP config is stored in .env, never in the code.

# .env (never commit this file) ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=sk-ant-... EMAIL_ADDRESS=your.address@icloud.com EMAIL_APP_PASSWORD=xxxx-xxxx-xxxx-xxxx # SMTP config for iCloud SMTP_HOST=smtp.mail.me.com SMTP_PORT=587 # For Gmail: smtp.gmail.com, port 587, same pattern
Part 5

5 The ATS Adapter

The tailored Markdown resume gets reformatted and validated for the specific ATS the company uses. One source, multiple output formats.
Why does this exist?
ATS platforms don't just store resumes — they parse and score them. Greenhouse rewards keyword density. Ashby looks for explicit criteria coverage. Lever does full-text search and penalizes unexpanded acronyms. A single static resume doesn't optimize for all three. This layer produces format-specific variants from the tailored Markdown source — without changing any facts or the voice.

Inside the adapter — 5 stages

Stage 1
Detect ATS
Read the company's ATS field from the hitlist. Branch into the correct format profile.
Out: ashby | greenhouse | lever | custom
Stage 2
Load Profile
Each ATS has a YAML profile: format rules, keyword strategy, acronym policy, summary style. No code changes to add a new ATS.
Out: rules object
Stage 3
Rewrite MD
Second Claude pass: rewrite the summary, expand acronyms, reorder bullets per the ATS profile. Facts unchanged — only formatting adapts.
Out: resume.<ats>.md
Stage 4
Render
MD → DOCX via python-docx. DOCX → PDF via LibreOffice headless. Single column. No tables. Standard fonts.
Out: resume.pdf + resume.docx
Stage 5
Validate
Round-trip check: extract text from PDF with pdfplumber, compare to source. Block if parse fidelity <90%.
Out: ats-report.json
Why a second Claude pass (Stage 3)? The first pass (Part 3) chose content. This pass chooses format. Separating them keeps each prompt small and focused. If Ashby changes its scoring criteria, you update the profile YAML — you don't touch the tailoring prompt. The two concerns don't bleed into each other.

ATS profiles — the rules each renderer follows

Each profile is a YAML config file. The rules are knowledge, not logic — stored in config, not code.
Rule Ashby Greenhouse Lever Custom
Output format
What the ATS parses most reliably		 DOCX preferred Text-based PDF DOCX preferred Both (PDF + DOCX)
Summary style
How the opening paragraph is scored		 Criteria-covering — explicitly address the role's stated hiring criteria Scorecard-mirroring — match JD nouns verbatim Keyword-dense — 12+ search-friendly terms JD-mirroring + conservative fallback
Acronyms Spell out once, then abbreviate Spell out once Always both forms: "Go-to-Market (GTM)" Always both forms
Bullet style Metric-first, criteria-explicit STAR format (Situation → Action → Result) Front-load keywords in first 10 words Metric-first, plain
Skills section size 12–16 items 12–18 items 15–20 items (used as search index) 12–16 items

The validator — automated pre-flight

Every rendered file passes an automated checklist. Failures block the file from being marked "ready to submit." You never see the checklist — you just see a green or red status in the dashboard.

Universal (all ATS)

  • Single column layout — parseable by all ATS parsers
  • No tables used for content (only tab stops)
  • No images, icons, or skill-bar graphics
  • Contact info in document body, not in header/footer
  • File size under 2 MB
  • Standard fonts only (Calibri / Arial / Georgia)
  • Consistent date format throughout
  • PDF round-trip parse fidelity ≥ 90%

ATS-specific

  • Ashby Summary covers ≥ 3 criteria from JD
  • Ashby No vague verbs that trigger "undecided" scoring
  • Greenhouse Top-10 JD keywords appear ≥ 2× each
  • Greenhouse Every bullet has a quantified result
  • Lever All acronyms spelled out at first use
  • Lever First 10 words of each bullet are keyword-dense
  • Custom Manual review flag — no public ATS docs
Why validate after render, not before?
It's tempting to check rules on the Markdown source and skip rendering if they fail. But the real failure mode is the render itself — a perfectly valid Markdown file can produce an unreadable PDF due to font issues or table rendering bugs. The validator runs on the rendered output because that's what the ATS actually receives.
Part 6

6 Networking CRM

A lightweight contacts tracker built into the dashboard. Not a full CRM — just enough to stay organized.
Why build this in instead of using a real CRM?
A separate CRM tool adds friction. The dashboard is already open when you're reviewing applications — adding contacts in the same place is one less context switch. The contacts data lives in a flat JSON file, which means it's version-controllable, portable, and readable by other scripts (like the AI tailor, which can check if you already know someone at a company).

Each contact has: name, title, company, LinkedIn URL, last contact date, status, notes. Status options: To Reach Out → Connected → In Conversation → Referred → Gone Cold.

When to add a contact

  • The AI tailor suggested them as a networking target
  • You found a mutual connection at a company you're applying to
  • You met someone at an event who works at a target company
  • Someone reached out to you about a role

Adding contacts without a running server

The dashboard's "Add Contact" form detects whether a local server is running. If not (the normal case — the dashboard is a static HTML file), clicking "Add" copies a terminal command to your clipboard:

python3 scripts/add_contact.py \ --name "[Name]" \ --title "[Title]" \ --company "[Company]" \ --linkedin "[URL]" \ --status "to reach out"

Paste in Terminal. The script appends to contacts.json and regenerates the dashboard.

8 Build your own — practical checklist

What you need to get this running from scratch. Roughly ordered by dependency.

Setup

  • Install Python 3.11+ and the dependencies: anthropic, requests, beautifulsoup4, python-dotenv
  • Get an Anthropic API key. Set it in .env.
  • Set up your email app password. Test SMTP manually before automating.
  • Create data/companies.json with at least 5 companies and verified ATS slugs.
  • Write your master resume in Markdown with tagged bullets. This is the highest-leverage thing you'll do — more bullets = more tailoring options for the AI.

Calibration (first week)

  • Run the scanner manually (python3 scripts/daily_scan.py) and check the output. Does it find real roles?
  • Read the first 3 cover letters. Do they sound like you? Adjust your master resume bullets and the prompt system message.
  • Check the fit notes. Are they honest, or is the AI being too positive about everything?
  • Tune the keyword and location filters based on what slips through (false positives) or gets missed (false negatives).
  • Set up launchd or cron. Let it run unattended for a week before trusting it.

Ongoing

  • Add the ATS adapter (Part 5) once the core pipeline is stable — don't build it first.
  • Audit the hitlist monthly. Remove companies that have frozen hiring or are no longer relevant.
  • Review the master resume quarterly. Add new bullets as you do new work. Archive old ones you no longer want to use.
  • Update ATS profiles if you notice your applications aren't getting through — profile rules can go stale as ATS platforms change their scoring.
The most common failure modes
1. Master resume is too thin. If you only have 2-3 bullets per role, the AI has no choices — every resume comes out the same. Write 8-10 bullets per role, with variety (different metrics, different themes). The AI will select; you just need options.

2. Too many companies on the hitlist. 30 companies sounds thorough. In practice, most of them don't have relevant roles most of the time. Start with 10-15 companies you'd genuinely be excited about. Add more as you learn what's active.

3. Treating AI output as final. The cover letters and fit notes are a first draft. The AI doesn't know your interviewer's priorities or the internal culture. Always read before submitting. The system saves you 80% of the time — you still do 20% of the judgment.
What this system is not: It is not a way to apply to 100 companies without effort. It is a way to apply to 10-15 companies at a quality level that previously would have taken 40+ hours of manual work. Volume is not the goal. Signal quality is.
